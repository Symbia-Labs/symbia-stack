When a total isn't the total

A guided tour of provenance lanes, using federal improper payments reporting.
Checking connection…

1 · The problem, in GAO's own words

Every year the federal government publishes an estimate of its improper payments — money that shouldn't have gone out, or went out in the wrong amount. For FY2025 that figure was $186 billion, across 64 programs at 15 agencies. Since FY2003 the running total is roughly $3 trillion.

Then, deeper into the same report, this:

"These estimates do not represent the full extent of government-wide improper payments. For instance, the $186 billion does not include certain programs that agencies have determined are susceptible to significant improper payments, such as the Department of Health and Human Services' Temporary Assistance for Needy Families." GAO-26-108694, published April 27, 2026
Read that carefully. The published government-wide total leaves out programs the agencies themselves identified as high-risk. The caveat is real, documented, and honest — and it lives in prose, in a report most people who quote "$186 billion" will never open. The number travels; the caveat doesn't.

This isn't a story about anyone hiding anything. It's a story about a number and its qualifier getting separated the moment the number is copied into a slide.

2 · What we built

A small data pipeline that assembles a government-wide total from individual program reports. The important part is one line of configuration — it declares in advance which programs must report for the total to be complete:

"expected": [
  "hhs.medicaid", "hhs.medicare-ffs", "treasury.eitc",
  "dol.unemployment-insurance", "sba.disaster-assistance", "hhs.tanf"
], "op": "sum"

Six programs. Reports flow in, get summed, and the total travels on to whatever consumes it:

program reports→rollup→convert to $B→audit log→dashboard
The one idea in this tour: every value that moves through this pipeline carries a lane — a claim about how far it can be trusted. canonical means it can be reconstructed and defended. apocryphal means it cannot. The lane is part of the value, not a note attached beside it.

Dollar figures per program in this tour are illustrative. The FY2025 aggregates in step 1 are real and cited. What's being demonstrated is the structure, not the arithmetic.

Stop here for a second. Five of six programs have reported. The total is $94.0 billion — a clean, plausible, board-ready number. Nothing on the face of it looks wrong. If you saw this in a briefing deck you would have no reason to question it.

But the pipeline is emitting it as apocryphal, and it names hhs.tanf as the reason. Not in a footnote — inside the value itself.

That is the exact situation GAO described in step 1, caught mechanically instead of editorially.
The gap closes. Same pipeline, same arithmetic, one more report — and the lane flips to canonical. Nothing was overridden or approved. The total simply became defensible, because every declared contributor is now accounted for.

4 · The part that matters most

An incomplete total gets flagged. Fine. But real systems don't stop there — the number gets divided, converted, averaged, and charted. The obvious failure mode is that a clean-looking result comes out the far end and the warning is lost in transit.

Here is the conversion step running on the incomplete $94.0B total:

{
  "result": 94,
  "expression": "94000 / 1000",
  "exact": true,
  "lane": "apocryphal",
  "receipt": { "kind": "recipe",
               "operation": "{value} / 1000",
               "inputs": { "value": 94000 } }
}
The math is exact and fully reproducible — and the value is still apocryphal. Correct arithmetic over an incomplete input does not repair the input. A downstream dashboard cannot quietly recover a trustworthy number by dividing an untrustworthy one.

Note the receipt survives the demotion. You get the full recipe — the operation and its inputs — so the calculation is auditable and correctly labelled as resting on incomplete ground. Those are two separate facts and the system reports both.

5 · Can an undeclared source move the number?

The expected list names six programs. What if a seventh reports — one nobody declared? If an unlisted contributor can push dollars into a total that still reads canonical, then the expected list is decoration.

Press the button to inject dhs.fema-public-assistance, a program not on the list, with a $7.5B estimate.

6 · What this does and doesn't prove

ShownNot shown
A partial total can be made structurally incapable of presenting as complete. That any of this is production-hardened. This ran in imagine mode.
The warning survives exact downstream computation. That the underlying program figures are correct — lanes describe assembly, not accuracy.
An undeclared contributor can't move a canonical total. Any ATO or accreditation posture. None of this is a control implementation.
The missing member is named inside the value, where it travels with it. That someone must act on the flag. It informs; it doesn't enforce policy.
Said plainly: the host this ran on describes itself as "in-memory, ephemeral keys, restart-lossy — a sketch, not a record." Enforcement is off. The signing keys don't outlive the process. A signed record of bad work is still a faithful record of bad work — provenance tells you where a number came from, never whether it was a good number.

The claim is narrow and worth stating precisely: a total that is missing declared inputs should not be able to present itself as whole. Today that's guaranteed by a footnote and a careful reader. Here it's guaranteed by the value's own structure.
